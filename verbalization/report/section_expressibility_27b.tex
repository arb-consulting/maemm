\subsection{Eliciting features that are difficult to verbalize}
\label{sec:expressibility}

A \method{} learns without supervision to write text that reproduces a direction, so we conjecture
that what it cannot write bounds what any natural-language readout of the activation, such as an
NLA, can express. A feature fails if none of $b{=}8$ samples clears the SAE's activation threshold
($\tau{\approx}1.6$); features that never fire in the corpus are excluded. Failure tracks sparsity
($74\%$ of the rarest decile of a rarity-weighted draw, $2.5\%$ of the densest;
\cref{app:verb-rare}), and training on failing features' own activating spans fixes $13$ of $171$
(\cref{tab:verb-transfer}).

Beyond rarity, failure follows form, not meaning (\cref{tab:verb-taxonomy}): $25$--$38\%$ of
features fixed by a position in a structure (templates, layout, formatted numbers, copies of
earlier text) fail, against $2\%$ of topic and $5\%$ of word features ($11\%$ overall). The text
exists, since an LLM shown $16$ corpus windows makes them fire; it is unreachable from the
activation alone. On these features the \method{}'s samples stay self-consistent (embedding cosine
$0.77$ vs.\ $0.80$ on passing features) but drift from the corpus ($0.51$ vs.\ $0.59$): a direction
conveys its meaning, not where in a structure it fires.
